A UK honours degree is awarded in one of four classes: First, Upper Second (2:1), Lower Second (2:2) or Third. At most universities the class comes from a weighted average of your module marks, with 70% for a First, 60% for a 2:1, 50% for a 2:2 and 40% for a Third. The exact weighting and borderline rules are set by each university, so your own regulations always have the final word.

## The four classes and their usual boundaries

Most UK universities use the same percentage boundaries. The University of Warwick and the University of Nottingham, for example, both publish these bands:

| Class | Short name | Usual weighted average |
|---|---|---|
| First-class honours | First, 1st | 70% and above |
| Upper second-class honours | 2:1, II.1 | 60% to 69% |
| Lower second-class honours | 2:2, II.2 | 50% to 59% |
| Third-class honours | Third, 3rd | 40% to 49% |
| Pass / ordinary degree (no honours) | Pass | Rules vary by university |

Below the Third, rules differ. Warwick awards a Pass degree without honours for an average from 35.0% to 39.9%. Nottingham lists an unclassified Pass, awarded without honours. Check your own regulations for what happens below 40%.

The Quality Assurance Agency (QAA) publishes descriptions of what each of the four classes should mean in terms of knowledge and skills. These are included in its *Frameworks for Higher Education Qualifications* (second edition, 2024). The descriptions are about the quality of work, not percentages. The percentage bands above are set by universities.

## How your class is calculated

### Which years count

In many three-year bachelor's degrees in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the first year must be passed but counts little or nothing toward your class. Your second and final years carry the weight. In Scotland, most honours degrees take four years, and the classification usually rests on the later years.

Each university decides how much each year counts. Nottingham, for example, says the relative weightings are set out in each programme specification. Warwick says the same about its course regulations. Many courses weight the final year more heavily than the second, but you need your own course handbook to know the split.

### Credit-weighted averages

Inside each year, modules are weighted by credits. A 40-credit dissertation counts twice as much as a 20-credit module. The steps are:

1. Multiply each module mark by its credits.
2. Add those up.
3. Divide by the total credits for the year.
4. Combine the year averages using your course's year weights.

### Rounding

Universities round differently. Warwick works with the mean mark to one decimal place, so 69.9% is a 2:1 and 70.0% is a First. Nottingham classifies borderline cases using rounded whole marks. Check whether your university rounds before or after the borderline rules apply.

## Worked example

Suppose your course weights the second year at 40% and the final year at 60%. (This is an example. Check your own regulations.)

### Second year (120 credits)

| Module | Credits | Mark | Mark × credits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Module A | 20 | 58 | 1,160 |
| Module B | 20 | 64 | 1,280 |
| Module C | 20 | 61 | 1,220 |
| Module D | 20 | 55 | 1,100 |
| Module E | 40 | 66 | 2,640 |
| **Total** | **120** | | **7,400** |

Second-year average: 7,400 ÷ 120 = **61.67%**

### Final year (120 credits)

| Module | Credits | Mark | Mark × credits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dissertation | 40 | 68 | 2,720 |
| Module F | 20 | 65 | 1,300 |
| Module G | 20 | 71 | 1,420 |
| Module H | 20 | 62 | 1,240 |
| Module I | 20 | 67 | 1,340 |
| **Total** | **120** | | **8,020** |

Final-year average: 8,020 ÷ 120 = **66.83%**

### Combining the years

Overall: (0.4 × 61.67) + (0.6 × 66.83) = 24.67 + 40.10 = **64.77%**, which rounds to 64.8%. That is a **2:1**.

### How much the weighting matters

The same marks give different averages under different weightings:

| Weighting (second : final year) | Overall average | Class |
|---|---|---|
| 50 : 50 | 64.25% | 2:1 |
| 40 : 60 | 64.77% | 2:1 |
| 25 : 75 | 65.54% | 2:1 |
| Final year only | 66.83% | 2:1 |

Here the class doesn't change, but the gap to a First narrows as the final year counts more. If you improved during your degree, a heavier final-year weighting helps you. You can enter your own modules and weights in the [UK degree classification calculator](/gpa/uk-degree-classification-calculator).

## Borderline marks and discretion

Your average is not always the end of the story. Most universities have rules for students who fall just below a boundary.

### Borderline zones

- **Warwick:** if your mean is below a class boundary but within 2.0 percentage points of it, the Board of Examiners reconsiders whether to move you up. This does not apply to the pass/fail boundary.
- **University of Surrey:** for 2025/26, Surrey treats results within two percentage points below the next boundary as borderline for Bachelor's and Integrated Master's students, with exclusions for its medicine and veterinary degrees.
- **Nottingham:** only rounded marks of 69, 59 and 49 are borderline.

### What examiners look at

When a mark is borderline, boards usually look at the pattern of your results, not just the average. Warwick lists:

- performance above the boundary in the final year,
- a preponderance of module marks above the boundary, and
- performance above the boundary in core modules.

The University of Edinburgh's School of Informatics lists similar factors, including improvement between years and the quality of your project. It stresses that the board decides whether to award the higher class, but your actual mark is not changed.

Nottingham's borderline process uses a fixed rule instead of discretion. A borderline student must be given the higher class if two-thirds or more of their final-stage credits, or of their final and penultimate-stage credits, are in the higher class.

### Example: a borderline First

Say your overall average is 68.4%. Under a two-point zone like Warwick's, that is within 2.0 points of 70, so the board would consider you. If 80 of your 120 final-year credits had marks of 70 or more, you would have a strong case. Under Nottingham's rounded-mark rule, 68.4% rounds to 68, which is outside the 69 borderline, so the two-thirds rule would not come into play. The same average can be borderline at one university and not at another.

## How employers and universities abroad read UK classes

### UK employers

Many UK graduate schemes have asked for a 2:1 as a minimum. Prospects Luminate reported in 2018 that just over three-quarters of graduates who entered a professional-level job had a 2:1 or a First, but also that graduates with a 2:2 or below still entered professional-level work. Requirements vary by employer and change over time, so check each job advert for whether a grade is "essential" or "preferred".

### Graduate schools and employers outside the UK

UK classes don't convert neatly to a GPA. A 65% average is a solid 2:1, but a straight-line conversion (65% of 4.0) gives 2.6, which looks weak to a US reader. That is why you should not convert it yourself.

Instead:

1. **Look for UK-specific requirements.** Many universities abroad list a UK equivalent directly on their international entry pages, such as "a UK 2:1 or equivalent".
2. **Send the original transcript** with your module marks and your university's classification rules.
3. **Use the service they name.** Some ask for a credential evaluation from a provider such as WES or ECE. Some German universities may convert your average with the modified Bavarian formula.

For a rough estimate on another scale, try the [GPA scale converter](/gpa/gpa-scale-converter), and label the result as an estimate.

## Common mistakes

- **Averaging modules without credits.** A 40-credit dissertation counts double.
- **Including first-year marks.** In many three-year degrees they count little or nothing. Check your course's weighting.
- **Assuming 69.5% is a First.** Only if your university rounds that way.
- **Counting on discretion.** Borderline rules are a review, not a guarantee.
- **Converting to a 4.0 GPA linearly** on applications abroad.
