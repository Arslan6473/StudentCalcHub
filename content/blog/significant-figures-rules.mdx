Significant figures are the digits in a measurement that carry real information: every certain digit plus the first uncertain one. Count all nonzero digits, zeros between them, and trailing zeros after a decimal point; never count leading zeros. When you calculate, adding and subtracting keeps the fewest decimal places, while multiplying and dividing keeps the fewest significant figures.

## Why significant figures matter

A balance that reads 2.00 g tells you the mass to the nearest hundredth of a gram. Writing "2 g" throws away that precision. Writing "2.0000 g" claims precision you never had.

Calculators don't know any of this. Divide 10.4 by 3.0 and your calculator shows 3.466666667. Sig fig rules tell you how many of those digits are meaningful.

## Rules for counting significant figures

1. **Nonzero digits are always significant.** 4.56 has 3.
2. **Zeros between nonzero digits are significant.** 305 has 3. 1.0072 has 5.
3. **Leading zeros are never significant.** They only place the decimal point. 0.0045 has 2.
4. **Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.** 100.0 has 4. 2.50 has 3.
5. **Trailing zeros in a whole number with no decimal point are ambiguous.** 1500 could have 2, 3 or 4. Most courses count it as 2. Write 1.500 × 10³ (or 1500. with a decimal point) to show all four were measured.
6. **In scientific notation, count only the number before × 10.** 6.02 × 10²³ has 3.

## How to round to a set number of significant figures

1. Count from the first nonzero digit to the number of sig figs you need.
2. Look at the next digit (the first one you'll drop).
3. If it's 5 or more, round up. If it's less than 5, leave the last kept digit as it is.
4. Replace dropped digits to the left of the decimal point with zeros, so the size of the number doesn't change.

| Round | To | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 2.3456 | 3 sig figs | 2.35 (dropped digit is 5, so round up) |
| 0.004682 | 3 sig figs | 0.00468 (dropped digit is 2) |
| 7.8951 | 2 sig figs | 7.9 |
| 1,549 | 2 sig figs | 1,500, better written as 1.5 × 10³ |

Some courses use a different tie-breaking rule for an exact 5, such as rounding to the nearest even digit. If your course does, follow it.

## Adding and subtracting: fewest decimal places

When you add or subtract, the answer can't be more precise than the least precise number, measured by **decimal places**, not sig figs.

### Example: adding masses

12.11 g + 0.3 g = 12.41 g on the calculator.

- 12.11 is known to the hundredths place.
- 0.3 is only known to the tenths place.

Round to the tenths: **12.4 g**.

### Example: subtraction can lose sig figs

25.624 mL − 25.1 mL = 0.524 mL on the calculator.

25.1 is known only to the tenths place, so round to the tenths: **0.5 mL**. Both inputs had at least 3 sig figs, but the answer has just 1. This is why small differences between large measurements are hard to measure precisely.

## Multiplying and dividing: fewest significant figures

When you multiply or divide, the answer keeps as many **sig figs** as the input with the fewest.

### Example: area

2.5 cm × 3.47 cm = 8.675 cm² on the calculator.

2.5 has 2 sig figs and 3.47 has 3. Keep 2: **8.7 cm²**.

### Example: division

9.84 g ÷ 2.1 mL = 4.6857… g/mL.

2.1 has 2 sig figs, so the answer is **4.7 g/mL**.

## Exact numbers don't limit sig figs

Some numbers aren't measurements. They have no uncertainty, so they're treated as having infinitely many significant figures and never limit your answer.

- **Counted values:** 3 coins, 25 students, 2 trials.
- **Defined conversions:** 100 cm = 1 m, 1 in = 2.54 cm, 1000 mL = 1 L.
- **Numbers in formulas:** the 2 in 2πr, the 100 in percent error, the 2 in 2(1.008 g) for the mass of two hydrogen atoms.

### Example

Each of 3 identical coins has a mass of 2.50 g. The total is 3 × 2.50 g = 7.50 g. The 3 is counted, so it doesn't limit the answer. 2.50 has 3 sig figs, so the answer keeps 3: **7.50 g**, not 8 g.

## Multi-step calculations

When a problem mixes operations, apply the rules step by step, but **don't round until the end**. Keep at least one extra digit in intermediate results, and track how many sig figs each step allows.

### Example: density by water displacement

A metal sample has a mass of **12.457 g**. The water level in a cylinder rises from **25.2 mL** to **35.6 mL**.

1. **Volume (subtraction):** 35.6 − 25.2 = 10.4 mL. Both are to the tenths, so 10.4 mL is good to the tenths. That's 3 sig figs.
2. **Density (division):** 12.457 ÷ 10.4 = 1.19779… g/mL.
3. 12.457 has 5 sig figs and 10.4 has 3. Keep 3: **1.20 g/mL**.

Keep the trailing zero. 1.20 shows 3 sig figs; 1.2 would show only 2.

### Example: subtraction then multiplication

(4.52 − 4.3) × 2.105

1. 4.52 − 4.3 = 0.22. 4.3 is only good to the tenths, so this difference has just 1 sig fig. Note that, but keep 0.22 for the next step.
2. 0.22 × 2.105 = 0.4631.
3. The difference has 1 sig fig, so the answer gets 1: **0.5**.

If you had rounded 0.22 to 0.2 first, you would get 0.2 × 2.105 = 0.421, which rounds to 0.4. Early rounding changed the answer.

## Quick reference

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Counting | Nonzero, sandwiched zeros and trailing zeros after a decimal count. Leading zeros never do. |
| Adding / subtracting | Keep the fewest decimal places |
| Multiplying / dividing | Keep the fewest significant figures |
| Exact numbers | Never limit the answer |
| Multi-step problems | Carry extra digits, round once at the end |

## Common mistakes

- **Counting leading zeros.** 0.0045 has 2 sig figs, not 5.
- **Dropping meaningful trailing zeros.** If the balance says 2.00 g, write 2.00 g.
- **Using the wrong rule.** Decimal places for + and −, sig figs for × and ÷.
- **Rounding at every step.** Round once, at the end.
- **Letting exact numbers limit the answer.** "3 trials" doesn't make your answer 1 sig fig.
- **Trusting the calculator display.** 3.466666667 is not a result you can report from 10.4 ÷ 3.0. The answer is 3.5.
