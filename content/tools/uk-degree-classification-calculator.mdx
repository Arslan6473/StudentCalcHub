## How it works

UK bachelor's honours degrees are classified as a **First**, **Upper second (2:1)**, **Lower second (2:2)**
or **Third**. Most universities turn your module marks into one overall average and compare it with the
usual boundaries:

| Classification | Overall average |
| --- | --- |
| First-class honours (1st) | 70 or more |
| Upper second-class honours (2:1) | 60–69 |
| Lower second-class honours (2:2) | 50–59 |
| Third-class honours (3rd) | 40–49 |
| Below honours | below 40 |

The calculator works in three steps:

- **Year average:** for each year that counts, multiply each module's mark by its credits, add those up,
  and divide by the year's total credits. A 40-credit dissertation counts twice as much as a 20-credit module.
- **Weighted overall average:** combine the year averages using your university's year weightings. Only
  the ratio matters, so 1 : 2 and 33.3/66.7 give the same answer.
- **Classification:** find the band the overall average falls in.

A final year counting twice as much as second year (1 : 2) is one common example, but it is not a rule.
Other universities use splits such as 25/75, count only the final year, or work out more than one method
and give you the better result. First-year marks often don't count at all, but check your handbook.

## Worked example

Your second year has three 40-credit modules marked **58**, **64** and **61**. Your final year has a
40-credit dissertation marked **72** and two 40-credit modules marked **66** and **69**. Your
university weights Year 2 : Year 3 as 1 : 2.

1. Year 2 average: (40 × 58 + 40 × 64 + 40 × 61) ÷ 120 = 7,320 ÷ 120 = **61**.
2. Year 3 average: (40 × 72 + 40 × 66 + 40 × 69) ÷ 120 = 8,280 ÷ 120 = **69**.
3. Weights 1 : 2 mean Year 2 counts for 1/3 and Year 3 for 2/3.
4. Overall average: 61 × 1/3 + 69 × 2/3 = 20.33 + 46 = **66.33**.
5. 66.33 is at least 60 and below 70, so the likely result is an **Upper second (2:1)**.

If the same university counted only the final year, the average would be 69 — one mark under the First
boundary. That puts you in the **borderline zone** many universities use, where a further rule or the exam
board decides whether you move up.

## When to use this

- To estimate your degree class part-way through your course, using the marks you have so far.
- To see what final-year average you need for a 2:1 or a First, by trying different marks.
- To check how much a year weighting, or a heavy dissertation, changes your result.
- To compare a UK class with other systems — see the [grade converter](/gpa/grade-converter) for the US
  4.0 scale.

## Common mistakes

- **Averaging module marks without credits.** A 40-credit module counts twice as much as a 20-credit one.
- **Assuming every university uses the same algorithm.** Each university publishes its own rules. Some
  leave out your lowest-scoring credits, round averages (is 69.5 a First?), use preponderance rules based
  on how many credits are in each class, or apply a borderline zone of about 1.5–2 marks. Your university's
  regulations always decide.
- **Counting first year when it doesn't count.** Many courses only require you to pass first year.
- **Treating a borderline result as guaranteed.** Being just under a boundary means you *may* be moved
  up, not that you will be.
