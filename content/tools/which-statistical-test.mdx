## How it works

Picking a test comes down to a few questions about your study:

1. **What's your goal?** Compare groups, measure a relationship, predict an outcome, or check counts
   against expected proportions.
2. **What kind of outcome?** Continuous numbers, ordinal ranks, or categories.
3. **How many groups, and are they paired?** The same subjects measured twice need a paired test.
4. **Is the data roughly normal, or is the sample large?** If not, a rank-based (nonparametric) test is
   the safer choice.
5. **For count tables, are the expected counts at least 5?** If not, use an exact test.

The chooser asks only the questions that matter for your path. You can click any earlier answer to go
back and change it.

## Worked example

You want to know whether students who use flashcards score differently on a quiz from students who reread
their notes. Thirty-five students are in each group.

1. Goal: **compare groups**.
2. Outcome: quiz score, which is **continuous**.
3. **Two groups**, made of **different students**, so they're independent.
4. 35 per group is more than 30, so the t-test is reasonable.

Suggested test: an **independent t-test**. Welch's version is a safe default because it doesn't assume
equal variances.

## When to use this

- Planning the analysis for a lab report, capstone or thesis.
- Checking your choice on a statistics homework problem.
- Before you calculate a [sample size](/statistics/sample-size-calculator), since the test you plan to use
  affects how many subjects you need.

## Common mistakes

- **Using an independent test for paired data.** Before/after scores on the same students need a paired
  test.
- **Running many t-tests instead of ANOVA.** Comparing three groups two at a time inflates the chance of
  a false positive.
- **Using chi-square with small expected counts.** Switch to Fisher's exact test.
- **Treating ranked ratings as exact measurements.** A 1–5 rating is ordinal; rank-based tests fit it
  better.
- **Reading correlation as causation.** A strong r shows that two things move together, not that one
  causes the other.
